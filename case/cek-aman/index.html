Case study

Cek Aman — reading a scam before the money moves

An Indonesian anti-scam app. Instead of a single yes-or-no verdict, it pulls together many weak, unreliable signals about a link, phone number, bank account, seller, OJK-registered lender, email, or QR code, and turns them into a cautious risk read with plain-language advice. It also reads screenshots of suspicious chats and reasons over the messy text it manages to extract. Available as an Android app and on the web.

Why it is built this way

The hard part of an anti-scam tool is not painting a green or red badge — it is that every input is ambiguous and every source of truth is partial, third-party, and sometimes wrong. A tool that sounds confident when it should not is worse than no tool at all: it teaches people to trust the wrong thing at the exact moment they are about to lose money. So the whole design is about staying useful while being honest about uncertainty, and never crossing the line into accusing a specific person.

1. Judging scam risk without a paid model

The AI features read a screenshot of a suspicious chat and reason about what it says. Done the obvious way, that means an expensive vision-plus-LLM call on every single check.

Why this is hard An anti-scam app is used most by people who will not pay for it, and each check delivers small, one-off value while the model bill is very real. A premium-model cost per check makes a free tier impossible. And the judgement here is not classification you can hardcode — it is reading intent in fuzzy Indonesian text: phishing, requests for an OTP or PIN, prize bait, illegal lenders, paid-task scams, "transfer first" deals.
How this was handled The image-to-text step runs on a server-side tesseract binary, not a paid vision API, so it costs nothing per call. The reasoning step goes to DeepSeek (deepseek-chat) — a low-cost, OpenAI-compatible model, reusing a key already paid for by another product — not a frontier model. The model is pinned to return strict JSON (a score, a risk level, a short list of signals, a summary, advice, and a readable flag) at low temperature, so its answer drops into the same result shape as the non-AI checks. DeepSeek is cheaper and weaker than a frontier model. The mitigation is a tight system prompt, hard caps on input length and number of signals, and an explicit readable=false path: when the extracted text is too garbled to judge, the app says it could not read it instead of inventing a verdict.

2. Reading scam screenshots, then reasoning over the mess

People do not paste text — they screenshot a WhatsApp chat, an SMS, a transfer receipt. The app has to turn pixels into words, then reason about whatever fell out.

Why this is hard OCR output is noisy: wrong characters, broken line breaks, Indonesian and English mixed together, emoji, redactions. A scam judgement built on a bad transcription inherits every OCR error, and the failure is silent — garbled text can still produce a confident-looking score. On top of that, the OCR binary might not even be installed on the machine.
How this was handled The pipeline treats OCR as fallible on purpose. A missing tesseract binary degrades to a clean 503 rather than a crash, so the rest of the app keeps working. Recognition runs in ind+eng because these screenshots are almost never a single language. Low-quality extractions are handed to the model with an instruction to return readable=false and a neutral score instead of a guess. Reasoning over OCR text will always be weaker than reasoning over the original message. The honest posture is to surface that uncertainty as a "text too unclear to assess" state rather than hide it behind a number.

3. Many different signals, every source unreliable

A single "is this safe?" answer is assembled from very different checks: a URL against Google Safe Browsing and a trusted-domain allowlist, a phone number, a bank account number, a seller or marketplace handle, an OJK-registered lender name, an email address, and a QR or QRIS code decoded straight from its raw EMVCo payload — plus community reports.

Why this is hard None of these sources is authoritative or always available. Safe Browsing can be unconfigured or time out. The OJK lender list is a snapshot that goes stale. Matching a name against that list is a trap: fuzzy matching would happily match an impersonator — a fake dana-instan-cepat onto a real registered lender — which is exactly the mistake that gets someone scammed. Community reports are sparse and easy to game. So every signal is weak on its own, and combining them naively manufactures false confidence in both directions.
How this was handled Scoring is deterministic, rule-based and stateless: it starts from "relatively safe" and subtracts a penalty for each real signal, so the result is explainable and a missing source simply contributes nothing instead of breaking the check. Every third-party source degrades gracefully — with no Safe Browsing key the check falls back to heuristics and reports and still returns. The OJK match is deliberately strict: an exact alias or a known brand suffix, never fuzzy, so an unknown name stays "unknown — be careful" and is never promoted to a false "registered". And across the whole product the output is a risk read plus official reporting links — never a claim that a specific person is a scammer. The strict, additive approach under-claims: it will say "not enough data" rather than guess. For a tool people reach for when they are about to lose money, under-claiming is the safe error.

Constraints that shaped it

  • Free by default, cheap to run. The people who need it most will not pay, so every per-check cost — OCR, model, third-party APIs — had to be near zero or degrade to free.
  • Every source belongs to someone else, and is partial. Safe Browsing, OJK lists, product databases, community reports — all third-party, all sometimes missing, so none of them can be a hard dependency.
  • Confidence is a liability. An anti-scam tool that sounds sure when it is not teaches people to trust the wrong signal, so under-claiming is the safe failure mode.
  • It must never accuse. The output is risk and education, not a legal verdict on a person — a line drawn before any feature, not after.
