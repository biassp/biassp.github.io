Case study

Selingkuh Detector — observing, without accusing

A relationship digital-safety app for Indonesian users. It reads the notification activity on the phone it is installed on — through an Android NotificationListenerService — and turns that into a plain-language picture of when and how the device was used, rather than a verdict about a person. It ships with Google sign-in, a credit wallet and a referral program, delivered as an Android app with a web companion at penyidik.co.

Why it is built this way

This app sits in an emotionally charged place, and the hard parts are not the UI. The only real view a normal app gets into activity it does not own is an unreliable one; the data it collects is ambiguous and easy to over-read; and the whole thing must monetise without becoming a tool that accuses a person or gets gamed for free credits. So the design is built around two disciplines: be honest about what can and cannot be known, and make every value-bearing action server-authoritative.

1. Reading signals the app does not own

The core signal comes from an Android NotificationListenerService — the app watches the notifications other apps post, because that is the only window a normal app gets into activity outside itself.

Why this is hard A NotificationListenerService is a firehose of ephemeral, inconsistent payloads. Every messaging app formats its notifications differently and changes them between versions; one message can arrive as several updates to the same notification; group summaries collapse multiple chats into a single line; and the OS silently kills the listener under Doze or memory pressure and after a reboot. There is no stable schema and no guarantee you saw everything.
How this was handled The listener is treated as an unreliable stream, not a database. Each posting is normalised to (app, title, text, time, key) and de-duplicated on the notification key, so an edited or re-posted notification does not double-count; group-summary lines are dropped in favour of the individual entries behind them. The service is registered to re-bind after it is killed and to restart on boot, and the app tells the user in plain terms that it can only capture what arrives while it is running. Notification access is a permission the user must grant by hand in system settings and that the platform scrutinises heavily, and it can never produce a complete log. The honest posture is to present it as "what was seen, with gaps" — never as "everything that happened".

2. Observations, never an accusation

It would be easy — and wrong — to turn a pile of notifications into a confident verdict about a person. The responsible product refuses to.

Why this is hard The same data has a dozen innocent explanations, and a tool that renders "guilty" from ambiguous signals does real harm: it can end a relationship over a misread, or become an instrument of control. The engineering temptation in this category is to score and rank "suspiciousness" and sell certainty — which is exactly the feature that should not exist.
How this was handled The app summarises patterns and timelines — when activity happened, with which apps, how it clusters — and stops there. The optional AI summary runs on DeepSeek (a low-cost, OpenAI-compatible model, reusing a key already paid for by another product), pinned to describe what is observed in neutral language and never to conclude that a person is unfaithful. The framing throughout is consent- and reflection-oriented: a picture of a device's own activity, presented as observation, not proof. This deliberately under-delivers on the sensational promise the category invites — and that is the point. For a tool in this space, the safe failure mode is to inform, not to accuse.

3. A credit wallet and referral system that can't be gamed

The app is free to start, with paid credits for deeper features and a referral program that rewards both sides — which immediately invites abuse.

Why this is hard A referral bonus is free money to anyone who can fake the other end: self-referrals from a second account, farmed signups that never become real users, replaying a link to mint credits, or racing two redemptions at once. And a balance the client is trusted to report is a balance that gets edited.
How this was handled The wallet is server-authoritative: the client never sets its own balance, and every debit and credit is a server transaction tied to a verified account through Google sign-in, so a tampered app cannot grant itself credits. Referral rewards are attributed once, on a qualifying event rather than on the click, with self-referral and duplicate attribution guarded server-side; the referral flow was regression-tested end to end before release. Server-authoritative accounting means anything touching the balance needs connectivity, and the anti-abuse rules occasionally hold a legitimate reward until its qualifying event. Paying rewards slowly and correctly beats paying them instantly and being drained.

Constraints that shaped it

  • A sensitive category, reviewed strictly. The app lives in a space app stores scrutinise, which shaped the listing, the launcher name and a review-only demo account — decisions made before any feature, not after.
  • Free to start, cheap to run. Casual users will not pay, so signal capture is on-device and the AI summary uses a low-cost model — paid credits buy depth, not the basics.
  • Incomplete data by design. Notification access can only ever show a partial picture, so the product is built to be honest about gaps rather than imply a complete record.
  • It must never accuse. The output is observation and reflection, not a verdict on a person — a line drawn before any feature, not after.
