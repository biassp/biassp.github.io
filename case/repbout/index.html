Case study

RepBout — workout battles, with money inside

An Android app where two people challenge each other on a set of exercises. What makes it more than a fitness app is the economy inside it: a gems balance, paid orders, and withdrawals that flow out into the real world. Once money is involved, every assumption changes.

RoleBuilt solo, app and backend

StatusIn progress · signed release

Two problems that shaped the product

An ordinary fitness app only needs to be roughly right — miscounting reps costs no one anything. RepBout doesn't have that luxury: a workout result decides who wins, and winning moves a balance that can be withdrawn.

1. The gems ledger and the order cycle

Every user has a gems balance. It grows through paid orders, shrinks when spent on a match, and moves when someone wins. Orders have a cycle of their own: created, paid, or expired.

Why this is hard A balance must never be created from thin air. That sounds trivial until you count the paths that can break it: a payment confirmed twice, an order that expires at the same moment its payment lands, a match that finishes twice because a connection dropped and the request was retried, or two requests arriving at once and both reading the old balance before either one writes. Every single one of those paths, if it goes wrong, creates money.
What makes it different from an ordinary feature A display bug can be fixed tomorrow. A ledger bug has already paid someone. And once the balance can be withdrawn, the mistake can't be undone with a press of undo — just like an inventory or accounting system, the only safe way is to make the balance derived from the record of transactions, not stored as a number that can be overwritten.
How this was handled Two safeguards, aimed at two different layers. The company vault — the fiat side that holds real money — derives its balance from an append-only ledger: revenue in, payouts out, never a figure that can be overwritten. The user gem balance is a server-authoritative field guarded by an idempotency key on every credit, so the same event arriving twice — a re-sent payment webhook, a match retried after the connection dropped — credits gems only once. Honest about the seam: the gems column is still a stored number, not yet summed from an append-only gem ledger — that migration is the intended next step. The append-only-and-derive principle is already live where it matters most, the vault, and in Gudang, where the stock balance is never stored either.

2. Verifying workout results that are easy to cheat

Two players compete on a set of exercises. The system has to decide who won.

Why this is hard There's no referee. The one reporting is the one who benefits from their own report — and once the result is worth money, some people will try. Typing "100 push-ups" is free. Phone sensors can be fooled by shaking the device. A video can be looped, sped up, or taken from someone else. Every verification mechanism has a cost on the other side: the stricter it is, the more it burdens honest people, and a fitness app that's annoying to use won't be used by anyone.
The problem attached behind it A player who quits mid-match. Does the stake burn, get refunded, or count as a loss? All three can be exploited if chosen wrong — and whatever the answer, it has to stay consistent with the ledger above, because every one of those decisions moves a balance.
How this was handled Verification lives entirely on the server and is graded in two tiers instead of one on/off gate. A submission that is physically impossible — a rep completed faster than a human body can move, more valid reps than the elapsed time can contain, a cadence past the sustained human ceiling — is hard-rejected outright. What is merely suspicious is not thrown away but soft-flagged: borderline-fast reps, a submission rate that looks like farming, and — the tell that catches a looped or replayed video — rep timings so regular that their coefficient of variation falls below what a real human ever jitters to. The result itself is never taken on the player's word: the server ignores the client-sent outcome and recomputes it against the authoritative ghost replay stored when the match was created, so there is no mutual-confirmation step to collude on. Flagged runs still resolve, but their replay is shadow-pooled — only ever matched against other flagged players — so a false positive costs a suspect nothing while a real cheat is quietly kept away from honest opponents. The judgement is deliberately asymmetric: hard-rejection is reserved for what is physically impossible so an honest player is never wrongly locked out, and everything ambiguous degrades softly rather than blocking — because in a fitness app a verification layer that punishes the honest majority kills the product faster than any cheater does. This is the part still most in progress.

The constraints that shaped it

  • Money makes everything permanent. A withdrawal can't be reversed, so the ledger's correctness has to be protected before features, not after.
  • Opponents with an incentive. Unlike an ordinary fitness app, here some users have a real reason to lie.
  • Verification has a price. Every anti-cheat layer adds friction for the honest majority; there's a point where the product dies from being too strict.
  • The release key is kept separate. The signing keystore doesn't live in the app repository — it's stored encrypted and apart, with a passphrase that is never stored alongside it.
